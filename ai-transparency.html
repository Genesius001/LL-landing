AI Transparency

Lucky Loki makes synthetic media. This page explains what that means in plain terms, what the law now expects when you publish it, and what we ask of everyone who uses the Services.

Last updated: 2026-08-13 Applies to: all Lucky Loki Services

What Lucky Loki produces

Lucky Loki replaces a face in a photo or a video clip with a different face, using artificial intelligence. The result is synthetic media: an image or a video that looks like a record of something real but is not. Nobody stood where the result shows them standing. Nobody did what the result shows them doing.

Under EU law, content of this kind falls within the definition of a deepfake — AI-generated or manipulated image, audio or video content that resembles existing persons, objects, places, entities or events and would falsely appear to a person to be authentic or truthful. That framing is not a judgment about your intentions. It is simply what the output is.

The practical consequence. Because a swap is indistinguishable from a photograph to the person looking at it, the burden of telling them otherwise falls on whoever publishes it. In most cases, that is you.

What the law expects

This is a summary for orientation, not legal advice, and it is not exhaustive. Rules in this area are changing quickly and vary by where you are and where your audience is.

European Union

Article 50 of the EU AI Act sets transparency obligations for synthetic content. It has two limbs that matter here. Providers of systems that generate synthetic content must mark that content in a machine-readable format so it is detectable as artificially generated or manipulated. Separately, anyone deploying a system to generate or manipulate deepfake content must disclose that the content has been artificially generated or manipulated, in a clear and distinguishable manner, at the latest at the time of first interaction or exposure.

Where content is part of an evidently artistic, creative, satirical or fictional work, the disclosure obligation is narrowed rather than removed: the existence of the manipulation must still be disclosed, in a way that does not spoil the work.

The disclosure obligation that falls on you as the publisher has applied since 2 August 2026.

United States

There is no single federal labeling rule, but there is a growing patchwork that reaches this kind of content. At federal level, the TAKE IT DOWN Act criminalizes the publication of non-consensual intimate imagery including “digital forgeries”, and requires covered platforms to operate a notice-and-removal process. At state level, laws on digital replicas, election-related synthetic media, deceptive deepfakes and biometric data create obligations that differ from state to state, several of which allow individuals to sue directly.

Platforms

Independently of the law, the major social platforms and both mobile app stores have their own rules requiring AI-generated media to be identified. Publishing an unlabeled swap can cost you a post, a monetization status or an account even where no law has been broken.

What we ask of you

  1. Get permission before you create. Explicit permission, from every identifiable person in the result, for this specific kind of use. This is set out in full in our Acceptable Use Policy.
  2. Disclose when you publish. Tell your audience that what they are looking at is AI-generated, in a way they will actually notice.
  3. Use the platform's own tool where one exists. Most platforms now have an “AI-generated” toggle at upload. Use it. It is more robust than a line in a caption, and it satisfies the platform's rules as well as informing your audience.
  4. Do not strip provenance information. If a file carries metadata, a label or a watermark identifying it as synthetic, leave it in place. Removing it is a breach of our Acceptable Use Policy and, in some jurisdictions, of the law.
  5. Keep your paperwork. For commercial work, retain the releases and licenses that cover the use. If someone challenges the asset later, that file is your answer.

What a good disclosure looks like

A disclosure works when a reasonable viewer would notice it before or as they take in the content — not after. In practice that means at least one of the following, and preferably more than one.

  • The platform's native AI-content label, applied at upload
  • A visible on-screen or in-frame marker on the asset itself
  • A clear statement at the start of the caption or description, not buried at the end
  • Provenance metadata preserved in the file

A disclosure does not work when it is in a link, in a bio, in a comment, in small grey text at the end of a long caption, or phrased so vaguely that the audience would not understand the content is synthetic.

Who has to do what

Article 50 splits the duty in two, and the half that matters most to you is the half that sits with you.

  • Marking the file is a duty on the provider of the system — a technical measure applied to the output so that software can detect it as artificially generated.
  • Telling the audience is a duty on whoever deploys the system to produce a deepfake, and on whoever publishes the result. That is you, and it is not discharged by anything the file does or does not carry.

So even where a file is marked, you still disclose it yourself, in the ways described above. Marking is for machines and for platforms; disclosure is for the person looking at your post.

If you are checking a file you received. The absence of a visible label does not mean a piece of media is authentic. Synthetic media is easy to produce and provenance data is easy to strip. Treat any image or video whose origin you cannot verify with the same caution you would apply to an anonymous document.

If you are a business user

Advertising, broadcast and client work bring additional duties that fall on you as the publisher, and increasingly on your client too.

  • Label the finished asset in the form the destination market requires. Some jurisdictions specify wording and placement for advertising that features synthetic performers or AI-modified footage.
  • Record which assets in a campaign are synthetic, and keep that record with the campaign file.
  • Check whether a digital replica of a performer needs separate consent, representation or compensation in the territory concerned.
  • Do not use synthetic media in political or election-related communications. Our Acceptable Use Policy prohibits this outright, and the rules differ in almost every jurisdiction that has them.
